# Problem Discovery

## 1. Problem Context

Software Engineering students are exposed to many software engineering concepts and practices throughout their education. These concepts are often taught across separate university courses, assignments, and learning resources.

While students may understand individual concepts such as requirements engineering, system analysis, software design, testing, and software development, applying these concepts together when building a real software project can be challenging.

The difficulty becomes more visible when students move from learning individual topics to working on a complete project and are expected to decide what to do, in what order, what each stage should produce, and how the different stages and artifacts relate to one another.

---

## 2. The Problem

Software Engineering students often learn software engineering concepts across separate university courses and learning resources, but may struggle to connect this knowledge into a structured, end-to-end process when building a real software project.

As a result, students may know how to code and understand individual engineering concepts, yet lack confidence in knowing:

* What to do first and what should come next.
* What each project phase should produce.
* How requirements, analysis, design, development, and testing are connected.
* Whether important engineering activities or considerations have been missed.
* Whether their engineering decisions are appropriate for the project.

---

## 3. Pain Points

### 3.1 Uncertainty About Where to Start

Students may know that they need to build a software project but may not know how to turn a project idea into a structured engineering process.

### 3.2 Unclear Next Steps

After completing one stage, students may be uncertain about what should happen next or whether they have completed the current stage sufficiently before moving forward.

### 3.3 Disconnected Knowledge

Students may possess knowledge from different Software Engineering courses but struggle to understand how these concepts work together within one real project.

### 3.4 Lack of Confidence in Engineering Decisions

A student may be able to make a system work but still be unsure whether the requirements, analysis, design, architecture, documentation, or testing approach is appropriate.

### 3.5 Difficulty Validating Engineering Quality

Checking whether a system works is different from determining whether it was engineered appropriately.

Students may find it difficult to identify issues related to areas such as:

* Requirements completeness
* Design quality
* Maintainability
* Security
* Testing coverage
* Documentation
* Consistency between project artifacts

### 3.6 Team Misalignment

In team projects, different members may develop different interpretations of requirements, system behavior, or design decisions when project information is not clearly documented and connected.

---

## 4. Root Causes

### 4.1 Fragmented Learning

Software Engineering knowledge is often distributed across multiple courses, assignments, books, tutorials, and other learning resources.

Students learn individual pieces of the discipline, but may not have enough opportunities to practice connecting these pieces throughout the lifecycle of one complete software project.

### 4.2 Gap Between Theory and Application

Understanding a Software Engineering concept does not necessarily mean knowing how to apply it as part of a real project.

There is a difference between:

> Understanding a concept

and

> Applying that concept as part of a structured engineering workflow.

### 4.3 Lack of Continuous Project Context

When concepts are learned through separate activities, students may not continuously see how one engineering decision affects later stages of the same project.

As a result, relationships between requirements, analysis, design, implementation, testing, and documentation may become unclear.

---

## 5. Impact

This problem can affect how students approach and manage software projects.

Potential impacts include:

* Starting development before adequately understanding the problem or requirements.
* Skipping or underestimating important engineering activities.
* Producing project artifacts that are not clearly connected.
* Repeating work because earlier decisions were unclear or incomplete.
* Spending significant time searching for guidance on what to do next.
* Becoming dependent on external sources for step-by-step direction.
* Having lower confidence in engineering decisions.
* Creating inconsistencies between team members' understanding of the project.
* Focusing primarily on whether the software works rather than whether it was engineered appropriately.

A key distinction is:

> **A working software product does not necessarily mean that the software was engineered well.**

---

## 6. Initial Problem Statement

> **Software Engineering students may learn many concepts throughout their education, but struggle to connect them into a coherent process when building a real project. They may know how to make software work, but lack confidence in whether their requirements, analysis, design, documentation, security, testing, and other engineering decisions are appropriate, complete, and connected.**
